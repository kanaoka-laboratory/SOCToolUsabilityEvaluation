# **Evaluation Criteria for SOC Tool Usability**

This document provides detailed explanations of the eleven usability evaluation criteria used in our expert based heuristic walkthrough method. These criteria were derived from empirical findings in prior SOC research, particularly observations regarding analysts' cognitive processes, alert handling challenges, and workflow requirements.

The criteria are grouped into two categories:

* **Criteria 1 to 8**: Usability aspects related to *during-task* interactions
* **Criteria 9 to 11**: Usability aspects related to *post-task reflection* and reasoning quality

Each criterion reflects a usability dimension that is particularly relevant to SOC operations, where analysts must handle large alert volumes, perform rapid triage, and make high stakes escalation decisions.

---

## **1. The number of displayed alerts is small**

Many SOC analysts report frustration with the sheer volume of alerts generated by SIEM systems. This criterion assesses whether the SOC tool keeps the number of displayed alerts at a manageable level. It reflects the extent to which excessive alert noise may hinder analysts' situational awareness and slow down task execution.

---

## **2. The amount of displayed alerts is appropriate**

Even when alert volume is high, analysts may still consider it appropriate if it accurately reflects the operational environment. This criterion evaluates whether the alert volume feels reasonable given the expected workload, acknowledging that high alert counts are common in SOCs but should still feel justified and interpretable.

---

## **3. The quality of displayed alerts is good**

SIEM alerts often contain richer contextual information than raw security tool outputs, but excessive or poorly structured information may overwhelm analysts. This criterion evaluates whether the displayed alerts provide meaningful, interpretable, and actionable detail without unnecessary noise.

---

## **4. Benign triggers are appropriately handled**

False positives are often divided into erroneous alerts and benign triggers. Analysts expect SIEM systems to provide mechanisms that help distinguish benign triggers from genuinely suspicious activity. This criterion assesses whether the SOC tool supports appropriate treatment of benign triggers, reducing unnecessary cognitive load.

---

## **5. No prior knowledge of the monitored environment is required**

Understanding the monitored environment is essential for accurate alert triage. This criterion evaluates whether the SOC tool provides sufficient contextual information so that analysts do not need deep, pre-existing knowledge of the environment to make correct judgments. It reflects the importance of environmental context for accurate escalation decisions.

---

## **6. The displayed information is sufficiently comprehensive**

Past studies note that SIEM alerts can be overly verbose while still lacking certain important details. This criterion evaluates whether the information presented on the screen is complete enough to support interpretation and further investigation, without requiring excessive navigation or external lookup.

---

## **7. There are no factors that cause redundant or duplicate operations**

When responding to true alerts, analysts must act quickly. Poorly designed interfaces can force analysts to repeat filtering, sorting, or navigation steps, slowing down triage. This criterion evaluates whether the SOC tool avoids unnecessary repeated operations that could impede efficient analysis.

---

## **8. Help and reference documentation are available and sufficiently detailed**

The availability of clear help information is part of established usability heuristics. In SOC operations, where rapid interpretation of complex information is essential, documentation and in interface explanations play an even more critical role. This criterion assesses whether helpful guidance is accessible and useful during real task execution.

---

## **9. When alerts indicating potential threats are shown, the entire process from detection to escalation can be completed using only the information displayed on the screen**

If analysts must frequently consult external resources or switch between interfaces to interpret alerts, their workflow becomes fragmented and slow. This criterion evaluates whether the SOC tool provides all necessary information on screen to support the full chain of analysis and escalation without external lookup.

---

## **10. Alerts that require attention and those that can be ignored can be clearly explained with valid reasoning**

For escalation decisions to be defendable, analysts must justify why an alert is significant or benign. This criterion assesses whether the SOC tool enables analysts to articulate the grounds for their decisions, based on clearly presented evidence rather than superficial display elements or ambiguous cues.

---

## **11. Alerts that require attention can be distinguished from ignorable alerts from an interface design perspective**

Interface design should help analysts differentiate important alerts from unimportant ones. Although many SIEM systems use color coding or severity labels, these cues are not always sufficient. This criterion evaluates whether visual distinctions between alert types are meaningful, consistent, and helpful for rapid triage.
